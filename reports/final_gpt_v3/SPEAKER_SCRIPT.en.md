# Media narratives and the consumer sentiment gap

Ten slides. Suggested duration: 7 minutes 25 seconds. English only.

## Slide 1. Media narratives and the consumer sentiment gap

Inflation can slow down while your shopping bill still feels expensive. That difference became central to this project.

I studied six questions about media bias and U.S. consumer sentiment before the 2026 midterms. I will focus on what we found, what changed when we looked more closely, and which explanations the evidence can support.

## Slide 2. What does the Michigan index measure?

The Michigan index combines five survey answers. Two describe current conditions: family finances compared with a year ago, and buying major household items. Three describe expectations for family finances and national business conditions.

For each question, the relative score is one hundred plus the favorable percentage minus the unfavorable percentage. The formula combines those five scores.

So the index captures how people experience the economy and what they expect next. That matters when we compare it with national economic statistics.

## Slide 3. RQ1. How can we measure bias in news and social media?

The biggest difference in our sample is emotional or mixed language. It appears in eighty-six percent of X posts and twenty-four percent of news headlines. Political blame is also more common on X.

But look at negative economic tone. The difference is much smaller: fifty-three percent against forty-five percent. More emotional language does not automatically mean more negative economic information.

This is why we measure tone, topic salience, framing, and political blame separately. Establishing bias also needs a benchmark, such as different coverage of the same economic release.

These results use two hundred and thirty eligible headlines and eight hundred and twenty-nine posts, across October 2019 to July 2026.

## Slide 4. RQ2. Did media depress U.S. sentiment before the 2026 midterms?

Here is the last month we can evaluate, August 2026. Actual sentiment was fifty-one point seven. The economy-only prediction was about sixty-six. Adding media brought it closer to sixty-one. Simply carrying forward the previous sentiment reading gave fifty-five point two.

The apparent gap changes substantially with the benchmark. We cannot call that gap the effect of media. Economic shocks could influence both coverage and consumers, and we do not know which respondents saw the sampled content.

July media predicts August sentiment here. The final weeks before the November election are outside our sample.

## Slide 5. RQ3. How much can unemployment, inflation and GDP growth explain?

The sentiment trend shows why this question matters. The average was about ninety-seven before the pandemic, compared with fifty-two in the latest three months. That is a decline of almost forty-five points.

Our descriptive model associates changes in unemployment, inflation and GDP growth with about seventeen points of that decline. Inflation accounts for most of that model-associated change.

Then we added accumulated price increases. The residual gap fell from about twenty-eight points to seven. A simple time trend left a larger gap of eighteen points.

The economic benchmark was missing something useful. That gives us a concrete alternative to assigning the unexplained difference to media.

## Slide 6. RQ3. Why can slower inflation still leave consumers squeezed?

The top chart shows the accumulated price increase. By our latest forecast date, prices were almost twenty-eight percent above December 2018.

The bottom chart shows inflation, which is the speed of that increase. It was about three point seven percent. Slower inflation still adds to an already higher bill. Real disposable income was growing by only about half a percent.

That makes continuing concern about purchasing power understandable. The price-level measure also improved forecasts in our economic models. It supports studying affordability, while personal reference prices would still be needed to establish anchoring.

## Slide 7. RQ4. Which perceptual mechanisms can our data actually measure?

We can measure the content proxies shown here. Negative economic tone is a proxy relevant to negativity bias. Topic salience measures attention to prices and relates to availability bias. Political blame relates to confirmation bias. Price-level framing relates to anchoring to past prices.

These labels describe the content. Measuring the mechanisms in people requires different evidence. Negativity bias needs responses to comparable good and bad news. Availability bias needs exposure and recall. Confirmation bias needs prior beliefs and belief updating. Anchoring to past prices needs individual reference prices and affordability measures.

Our strongest observation is the content difference. The psychological explanation remains a research question.

## Slide 8. RQ5. Does media improve predictions beyond economic indicators alone?

These are forecasting errors over the same forty test months. Lower is better.

With the economy and prior sentiment, adding media barely changes the error: both round to four point two. Adding income and price levels gives a similar result.

There is one promising case. With seasonality and survey-mode controls, media reduces the error from three point eight six to three point five three. The survey-mode control reflects Michigan's 2024 transition to web collection.

But that improvement reverses under the strictest coverage check, and its advantage over carrying forward prior sentiment remains uncertain. Adding every content feature also makes the error worse, at four point seven four. More text features did not solve the small-sample problem.

## Slide 9. RQ6. What evidence challenges a media-bias explanation?

Two findings challenge a strong media explanation. First, adding price levels substantially shrinks the economic model's residual gap. Second, the media forecasting gain changes with the coverage rule.

In this chart, negative values mean improvement. With the strictest rule, requiring three news headlines and five posts per month, the gain becomes an increase in error. That comparison also has fewer months, so it is a warning about stability.

The next useful steps are specific: compare coverage of the same economic releases, connect actual media exposure with survey responses, and measure household budgets alongside reference prices. Those comparisons would help distinguish framing, persuasion and affordability.

## Slide 10. Key Takeaways

Our clearest media finding is stronger emotional language and political blame on X.

Our clearest economic finding is that accumulated price pressure changes how much of the sentiment gap remains unexplained.

Our forecasting finding is conditional: media helps in some specifications, but the gain does not survive every harder comparison.

For sentiment forecasting, purchasing power and the latest sentiment reading deserve attention before we add more text features. The shopping bill turned out to be an important part of the research.
