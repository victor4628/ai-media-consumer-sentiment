# Media narratives and the consumer sentiment gap

## Slide 1. Media narratives and the consumer sentiment gap

Inflation can slow down while the shopping bill stays high. 
I studied six questions about media bias and U.S. consumer sentiment before the 2026 midterms. I will share what we found and what those findings tell us about the sentiment gap.

## Slide 2. What does the Michigan index measure?

The Michigan index combines five survey answers. Two describe current conditions: family finances compared with a year ago, and buying major household items. Three describe expectations for family finances and national business conditions.

For each question, we take one hundred plus the favorable percentage minus the unfavorable percentage. The formula combines the five scores into the index.

So the index tells us how people see the economy today and what they expect next. We can then compare that with the economic indicators.

## Slide 3. RQ1. How can we measure bias in news and social media?

We measure tone, topic salience, framing and political blame separately. 

We found that X uses much more emotional or mixed language in our sample: eighty-six percent, compared with twenty-four percent in news headlines. Political blame is also more common on X.

The gap in negative economic tone is smaller: fifty-three percent on X and forty-five percent in news. 

Our sample includes two hundred and thirty headlines and eight hundred and twenty-nine posts, from October 2019 to July 2026.

## Slide 4. RQ2. Did media depress U.S. sentiment before the 2026 midterms?

For August 2026, actual sentiment was fifty-one point seven. The economy-only model predicted about sixty-six. Adding media tone lowered the prediction to about sixty-one. Carrying forward the previous sentiment reading gave fifty-five point two.

So the size of the gap depends on which model we use. It does not tell us how much media lowered sentiment. The same economic shocks can affect both coverage and consumers, and we do not know whether the survey respondents saw these posts or headlines.

## Slide 5. RQ3. How much can unemployment, inflation and GDP growth explain?

Before the pandemic, sentiment averaged about ninety-seven. In the latest three months, it was about fifty-two. That is a fall of almost forty-five points.

Our descriptive model links unemployment, inflation and GDP growth to about seventeen points of that decline. Inflation accounts for most of those points.

When we added accumulated price increases, the unexplained gap fell from about twenty-eight points to seven.

So higher prices give us another explanation for the gap, beyond media coverage.

## Slide 6. RQ3. Why can slower inflation still leave consumers squeezed?

The top chart shows the accumulated price increase. By our latest forecast date, prices were almost twenty-eight percent above December 2018.

The bottom chart shows inflation. It was about three point seven percent. Prices are still rising from an already higher level. Real disposable income was growing by only about half a percent.

Adding accumulated price increases made our economic forecasts more accurate. This suggests that the higher cost of living helps explain why consumer sentiment remains low.

## Slide 7. RQ4. Which perceptual mechanisms can our data actually measure?

Negative economic tone is something we can count. To measure negativity bias, we would need to compare how people react to similar good and bad news.

Topic salience tells us how often prices come up. To measure availability bias, we would need to know what people saw and remembered.

Political blame is something we can identify in the text. To measure confirmation bias, we would need to know people's prior beliefs and how they changed.

Price-level framing shows attention to what things cost. To measure anchoring to past prices, we would need personal reference prices and affordability measures.

The chart shows differences in content. It does not establish these psychological biases in consumers.

## Slide 8. RQ5. Does media improve predictions beyond economic indicators alone?

These bars show forecasting errors for the same forty months. A lower number means a more accurate forecast.

Once the model includes the economy and prior sentiment, adding media tone barely changes the error. Both values round to four point two. Adding income and price levels gives much the same result.

With seasonality and survey-mode controls, adding media tone lowers the error from three point eight six to three point five three. The survey-mode control reflects Michigan's switch to web surveys in 2024.

The improvement reverses under the strictest coverage rule. We also cannot clearly say this model beats carrying forward prior sentiment. Adding all the content features raises the error to four point seven four. More features do not always give a better forecast.

## Slide 9. RQ6. What evidence challenges a media-bias explanation?

We found two reasons to question a strong media explanation. Adding price levels greatly reduces the economic model's unexplained gap. And the forecasting benefit from media changes with the coverage rule.

In this chart, negative values mean that media improves the forecast. With at least three news headlines and five X posts per month, the error goes up instead. Only thirteen months remain under that rule, so the conclusion depends on which months we can use.

To study this further, we should compare coverage of the same economic releases and link actual media exposure to survey responses. We should also collect household budgets and the past prices people use as a reference. This would help separate media framing from the pressure of higher prices.

## Slide 10. Key Takeaways

X contains more emotional language and political blame in our sample.

Our clearest economic finding is that accumulated price pressure reduces the unexplained sentiment gap.

Media improves forecasts in some models, but the improvement depends on the controls and coverage rule.

For forecasting, we should pay attention to purchasing power and the latest sentiment reading before adding more text features. The shopping bill turned out to be an important part of the research.
